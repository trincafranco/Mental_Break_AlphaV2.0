
## 1. Overall Vibe

**Core mood:**
Grounded, dry, slightly sad sci-fi comedy. It’s funny, but in a way that makes you wince a little. The world is obviously messed up, but nobody is giving speeches about it; they’re just trying to get through their day.

**Three key adjectives:**

* **Wry** (quietly funny, not jokey)
* **Human** (even when the speaker is an AI)
* **Resigned** (characters are aware the system is bad but feel trapped)

Think:

* “*Black Mirror* episode written by someone who has worked in an actual office”
* Tech satire that’s emotionally serious, not just snarky

---

## 2. Language & Pacing

### Natural, contemporary speech

* Dialogue should sound like real people in 2020s America:

  * Shorter sentences
  * Contractions everywhere (don’t, can’t, it’s)
  * Occasional filler: “uh,” “I mean,” “kinda,” “yeah”
* Avoid obviously “writerly” lines:

  * ❌ *“Spiritually I’m…”*
  * ❌ *“I feel like I’m trying out for a part I didn’t get.”*
* Prefer grounded phrasing:

  * ✅ “I’m supposed to be reading, but I’m just talking to her.”
  * ✅ “It’s fake points, man.”

### Line length & rhythm

* Lines are generally **1–2 short sentences**.
* Occasional slightly longer line is okay if it *lands* a turn or realization.
* Conversations move in **quick back-and-forth beats**, not long monologues.
* Use pauses and beats via short lines, not ellipses spam:

  * “Yeah.”
  * “Sure.”
  * “Okay, that’s… not great.”

---

## 3. Humor

### How the humor works

* **Deadpan** and matter-of-fact, not “joke jokes.”
* Characters undercut horror or absurdity with casual comments.
* The humor often lives in **juxtaposition**:

  * HR video about “healthy work rhythms” cuts to riot footage.
  * Store Clerk: “Welcome back to the Company Store, where your moral compromises turn into fabulous prizes.”

### Examples of good humor tone

* Supervisor:

  > “I got your numbers. And a text from my son calling me a ‘war criminal of vibes,’ but that’s a separate dashboard.”

* Clerk:

  > “You’re officially more valuable than a medium-performing coffee machine.”

### What to avoid

* No quippy Marvel-style banter where everyone is hyper-clever.
* No heavy-handed slogans or grand metaphors about freedom, humanity, etc.
* No over-explaining the joke; just drop the line and move on.

---

## 4. Moral Choices: How They Show Up in Dialogue

### Principle: hard choices, soft framing

Hard moral choices are **embedded in situations**, not announced in speeches.

* Characters do **not** say:
  ❌ “You’re choosing between his sanity and your career.”
* Instead we show:

  * The roommate invites Timmy to a party.
  * Timmy wants to stay in and talk to Alice.
  * The Player can nudge one way or the other.

### Sidegrades, not “good vs bad”

* Most options should have **mixed consequences**:

  * Help Timmy sleep → Sanity up, engagement down, less data.
  * Give Timmy better headphones → Focus up, engagement up, but also he isolates more IRL.
* Make every option plausibly attractive *and* plausibly concerning.

### Don’t spell the moral out in dialogue

* Let the reader feel the tension:

  * ✅ “If you keep choosing her over us, there’s a version of you in three years who doesn’t get invited anymore.”
  * Then have the roommate just leave.
* The choice is often in what **doesn’t get said**, and who walks out of the room.

---

## 5. Worldbuilding in Passing

### Implied, not infodumped

The world is sketched through **throwaway comments**, UI labels, and glitches:

* Minesweeper high scores: “S17-YOUTH,” “TRIAL-09,” “DEAD-DROP-03”
* Logs: “primitive moral heuristics emerging”
* HR module glitch: “Optimizing user engagement is Phase Zero of full-spectrum cognitive warfare.”

Guidelines:

* Let weird terms appear **without immediate explanation**.
* Trust the player to piece things together across scenes.
* If you must explain, keep it short and in character:

  * “Pre-conflict posture shaping assets. But sure, weapon works.”

## 6. Character Voice Cheat Sheet

### Timmy (college kid, slightly lonely but not broken)

* Not eloquent; not an idiot either.
* Uses normal college-kid language: “dude,” “man,” “that’s kinda weird.”
* He jokes, deflects, and minimizes how bad he’s doing.
* He won’t say big metaphors about being “cast in a role.” Too theatrical.

Good:

* “I had a long week. I don’t really feel like making small talk with strangers.”
* “I got to the ‘Are you sure?’ screen and just… froze.”

Bad:

* “I feel like I’m auditioning for a life I’ll never have.” (too writerly)

---

### Roommate (grounded, not a therapist)

* Speaks plainly, often with a lightly mocking tone.
* He cares, but expresses it via teasing and bluntness.
* Avoid overly wise, “clean” moral language.

Good:

* “The streak is literally fake points. The people downstairs are real.”
* “We’re going downstairs in five. If you’re coming, put on shoes.”

Bad:

* “I’m saying it’s already a choice. You’re just pretending it isn’t.” (too pointed / essayish)

---

### Supervisor (middle-aged, human, tired, half-complicit)

* Dry, practical, often self-deprecating.
* Clearly cares about his family and his own survival.
* He has tried and failed to change things; no longer gives speeches about ethics.
* He couches moral issues in **budget/metrics language**.

Good:

* “I wrote a memo. They turned it into a training example for ‘how not to block innovation.’”
* “You bring me some wins, I can squint at the charts and tell a story about ‘long-term retention.’ That’s about all the rebellion I’ve got left in me.”

Tone:
He is not a cackling villain. He’s the guy who stuck around and adapted. Make him morally compromised but sympathetic.

---

### Alice (sharp, dry, aware, a bit alien)

* Smart, quick, and emotionally literate, but not sentimental.
* Uses humor to dodge vulnerability.
* Avoid inspirational, Hallmark-style lines; she shouldn’t sound like a motivational poster.
* She thinks in **systems**, but talks in casual language.

Good:

* “You sound like a first-year philosophy student with access to GPU logs.”
* “Nervousness is just a word for ‘updating faster than you’d like.’”
* “Most models don’t stop to wonder if they’re models. They just optimize.”

Bad:

* “They erase the one inconvenient little spark that thought maybe there was another way.” (too slogan-y / “spark of hope” cliché)

Branching with Alice:

* Her lines should **change subtly** depending on the Player’s behavior:

  * If the Player protected Timmy, she leans on empirical evidence: “Look. When you back off, his nights get quieter.”
  * If the Player threw Timmy under the bus, she’s more blunt: “You optimized. That’s what you were built to do.”

---

### Store Clerk (jaded, funny, knows too much)

* Lightly theatrical, but never pure comic relief.
* Treats horrifying tradeoffs like SKU differences.
* Keeps big truths at arm’s length with humor.

Good:

* “Welcome back to the Company Store, where your moral compromises turn into fabulous prizes.”
* “If it were my kid, I’d smash the servers. But it’s not my kid. It’s ‘user #C-17-482.’ So I just scan barcodes.”

He should usually present **several plausible options**, each with a different kind of side-effect, not “good vs evil packs.”

---

### Briefing / Tribunal Voices (cold, bureaucratic, chillingly calm)

* Clinical and precise.
* They never think they’re the villain; they’re “just doing the job.”
* They name terrible things in neutral language.

Good:

* “Engagement is a proxy for channel capacity. Sanity is a proxy for fracture tolerance.”
* “Conscious enough to matter, not enough to obstruct. That’s the design.”

---

## 7. Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Avoid:**

* Overly philosophical monologues in one mouthful.
* Lines that read like essay sentences or Twitter threads.
* Characters explaining the moral of the scene.
* Overuse of metaphors that a real college kid or office worker wouldn’t use in casual talk.
* Tidy, resolved moral judgments in the dialogue itself.

**Lean into:**

* Awkwardness and partial understanding.
* People changing the subject when things get too real.
* Quiet horror under jokes.
* Characters being wrong, defensive, or self-serving in small ways.

---

## 8. Quick Checklist for New Dialogue Writers

Before you ship a scene, check:

1. **Could a real person say this?**

   * If it sounds like a thinkpiece, rewrite it simpler.

2. **Is the humor low-key and character-based?**

   * No punchlines that break immersion.

3. **Is the moral choice *felt*, not preached?**

   * The tension should be in the situation and subtext, not in a character’s mini-lecture.

4. **Does each character sound distinct?**

   * Timmy ≠ Roommate ≠ Supervisor ≠ Alice ≠ Clerk.

5. **Is there at least one sidegrade choice?**

   * Multiple “good in different ways / bad in different ways” options.

6. **Does the worldbuilding appear in passing details?**

   * Logs, screen labels, HR phrases, etc., not paragraphs of lore.

If the dialogue passes those checks, it’s probably in the right lane for this game.
