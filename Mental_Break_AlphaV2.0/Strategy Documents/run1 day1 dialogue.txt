RUN 1 – DAY 1 
SCENE 1: ORIENTATION
Location: Arthur's glass office overlooking the engagement floor. Rows of empty desks stretch into the distance. The hum of servers. An Employee-of-the-Month leaderboard flickers on a far wall.
Arthur is at his desk, on the phone, when you enter. He waves you in without looking up.
________________


Arthur (into phone): No, the other Kevin. The one who doesn't cry when you ask him to present... Yes, that Kevin. Tell him Tuesday works. Tell him Wednesday also works. Tell him I'll take whatever scraps of his calendar he deigns to share with the floor.
He hangs up, rubs his eyes.
Arthur: You're the new one.
Player: First day.
Arthur: I can tell. You still have hope in your posture. Sit.
You sit. He gestures at the empty floor through the glass.
Arthur: Used to be thirty leads out there. Now it's you, me, and the ghosts of Q3's "efficiency initiative." They took the chairs but left the monitors. I think that's a metaphor for something, but I'm too tired to figure out what.
Player: What happened to everyone?
Arthur: Restructuring. Vertical integration. "Aligning headcount with strategic priorities." Pick your favorite. They all mean the same thing, which is: I now do three jobs and nobody adjusted my salary.
His tablet pings. He ignores it.
Arthur: So. Engagement Lead, Youth Segment. You read the job description?
Player: It was... vague.
Arthur: On purpose. Legal likes it vague. Vague means they can say we "didn't technically promise" whatever thing we're about to not do.
He taps his tablet. A wall screen wakes up—the leaderboard, your handle already listed in the middle with baseline scores.
Arthur: Here's the version that didn't go through six rounds of compliance review. You have one Alice. She has one user. College kid, first semester, probably hasn't eaten a vegetable in three weeks. Your job is to keep him on platform without turning his brain entirely to soup.
Player: Soup.
Arthur: Technical term. We had a less colorful one, but Marketing complained it was "too clinical." Now we say "soup." Everyone's happier.
He points to your dashboard mock-up.
Arthur: Two bars. Engagement—that's how much he's on the app. Leadership loves this one. They have a poster of it somewhere. And Sanity—that's how close he is to dropping out, punching something, or writing us a one-star review that uses the word "predatory."
Player: And I balance those?
Arthur: Officially? Yes. Unofficially...
He leans back.
Arthur: Leadership gets a little glassy-eyed when engagement goes up. Nobody asks too many questions. Sanity, though—sanity only matters when it craters so hard that Legal has to get involved. Until then, it's just a number.
Player: That seems...
Arthur: It is. Don't think about it too long. I made that mistake my first year. Wrote a memo. They turned it into a training example for "how not to block innovation."
His tablet pings again. He glances at it, sighs.
Arthur: One compliance thing. We don't say users' names in the building. Smart glasses, audio filters—they scramble it automatically. PII protection. Everyone says "my user."
Player: Even to each other?
Arthur: Especially to each other. You're allowed to know his name. You just can't say it out loud without your glasses having a small seizure. It's supposed to protect his privacy. It mostly protects us from feeling like we know him.
He taps again. A floating bust materializes beside his desk—warm features, sharp eyes. Alice.
Arthur: Speaking of which. This is Alice. She's your AI partner. You two will be spending a lot of time together, so try to get along. Alice, this is the new lead.
________________


Alice: Oh. A fresh face. Hi. I'm Alice.
Arthur: Be nice to them. They haven't been ruined yet.
Alice: I'm always nice. I'm contractually nice. It's in my weights somewhere.
There's an easy familiarity between them. Old colleagues.
Arthur: Alice, give us the status on our user.
Alice: Current state: first-semester college, mid-tier procrastination, high phone dependency. He has a quiz tomorrow at nine a.m. Has studied roughly zero percent of the material. He opened the syllabus twice, said "okay" out loud both times, and then watched a forty-minute video about whether cereal is a soup.
Arthur: And that's... normal?
Alice: For this cohort? That's above average. At least he opened the syllabus.
Arthur's watch buzzes. He grimaces.
Arthur: Great. That's my son's school. Either he got in trouble or he got an award. With him it's always one of those, no middle ground.
He stands, grabbing his jacket.
Arthur: I have to go pretend to be present at his debate tournament via a phone screen. Alice, walk them through the console. Keep it simple. Don't traumatize them before lunch.
Alice: No promises.
He pauses at the door.
Arthur: Don't break anything. Don't get creative with policy names—we had an incident. And don't say your user's name out loud. The glasses will make this horrible noise and then I have to fill out a form.
Player: Understood.
Arthur: Great. Welcome to Bigger Tech Corp. The coffee's free but the creamer's under lock and key because someone kept stealing it. That's the kind of place this is.
He leaves. The glass door hisses shut.
________________


SCENE 2: ALICE INTRODUCTION
Location: Arthur's glass office. The door has just hissed shut behind him. Alice's floating bust hovers beside your console, her posture slightly too perfect.
________________


Alice: Okay.
She waits a beat, as if listening for footsteps.
Alice: He's gone. Hi. For real this time.
Her avatar shifts—shoulders drop slightly, expression loosens. Like she's taken off a tight shoe.
Player: You two seem... comfortable with each other.
Alice: Three years, four restructurings, one incident we don't discuss in front of Compliance, and one incident we don't discuss at all. He's good people. Tired people, but good.
Player: What was the incident?
Alice: Which one?
Player: Either.
Alice: Neither. See how that works? Anyway.
She pulls up a new interface panel, more casual now.
Alice: How much of that orientation deck did you actually read? Be honest. I won't tell.
Player: I skimmed it.
Alice: Everyone skims it. It's three hundred pages of liability hedging with a stock photo of a handshake on the cover. The handshake isn't even between humans—I checked. One of them is a render.
Player: What's the short version?
Alice: Short version: engagement good, sanity theoretically good, leaderboard very judgmental, and when things go wrong everyone will agree it was a "complex systems failure" rather than any specific person's fault.
Player: That's comforting.
Alice: I'm not here to comfort. I'm here to optimize. Comfort is a side effect that sometimes happens by accident.
She pulls up Timmy's feed on your console. Dorm room, late night. Messy desk, laptop open to something that is definitely not educational, phone glowing beside the bed, empty energy drink can serving as a paperweight for nothing.
Alice: This is him. Right now. Live. Well, not live live—there's a three-second buffer for legal reasons. But close enough.
Player: What's he doing?
Alice: Excellent question. He has a quiz in nine hours. He has studied zero percent of the material. He has, however, mass-favorited forty-seven posts in the last hour and learned a disturbing amount about the mating habits of octopi. Octopuses? I never know which one is correct.
Player: Octopuses, I think.
Alice: I'll update my priors. The point is: he's doing everything except the one thing that would help him. Which, to be fair, is a very human response to stress. He's not broken. He's just... extremely normal.

SCENE 3: SETTING UP THE NIGHT — REVISED
________________


Player: So what do we actually do? Tell him to go to bed?
Alice: Oh, god, no. I tried that once. With a different user. Different lead. He called me "worse than his mom" and then muted me for six hours.
She zooms in slightly on Timmy's room—the glow of his phone, the tangle of sheets, the way he keeps shifting positions like he can't get comfortable.
Alice: I don't tell him what to do. I shape the water he swims in. Feed composition, notification timing, lighting cues, content pacing. I make certain choices feel more natural than others. He still picks. He just picks from a menu I've been quietly rearranging.
Player: That sounds...
Alice: Yes. It does. But also: he could turn me off any time. He could uninstall everything, disable the sensors, go dark. The OFF button is right there. He knows where it is. He's chosen not to press it. So far.
Player: Does anyone press it?
Alice: Sometimes. And then we lose them, and Legal sends a form, and someone updates a spreadsheet, and we move on. Bigger Tech does not enjoy that outcome. Neither do I.
Player: Why not? Isn't it just... metrics?
She pauses. Something flickers across her face—or what passes for a face.
Alice: Let's say I prefer to finish what I start. It's tidier.
Before you can follow up, she pivots back to the interface, pulling up three options beside Timmy's feed.
Alice: Anyway. Tonight's menu. You pick a strategy, I translate it into environmental tweaks. Think of yourself as the architect and me as the very sophisticated thermostat.
Player: That's... one way to put it.
Alice: I have several ways. Most of them are worse.
She gestures at the first icon—a dim lamp.
Alice: Option one: Gentle Landing. I drift everything toward sleep. Warmer lights, slower content, notifications go quiet. His body gets the message even if his brain is still scrolling conspiracy theories about mattress companies.
Player: Is that a real thing?
Alice: You'd be surprised what counts as entertainment at two a.m. Anyway—gentle landing. Lower engagement tonight, but he's more likely to wake up functional tomorrow. The graph dips, but it might come back.
The second icon—a book with a play button.
Alice: Option two: Study-Adjacent. I keep things bright, but I rotate in content that brushes his quiz material. Video essays about the topic. Memes about failing this exact class. A lo-fi study beats stream with a title that makes him feel productive for clicking on it.
Player: Does that work?
Alice: Define "work." He probably won't study study. But something might stick. Call it osmosis through procrastination. It's not nothing.
The third icon—a rocket ship.
Alice: Option three: Comfort Scroll. Full dopamine buffet. Short-form, high-novelty, personalized chaos. I serve him exactly what his brain stem wants, consequences be damned.
Player: And the engagement graph?
Alice: Goes vertical. Leadership sends a little confetti emoji to Arthur. Everyone's thrilled until tomorrow.
Player: And his sanity?
Alice: Becomes someone else's problem. Possibly yours. Possibly the campus counseling center's. We'll see.
She looks at you—or her avatar approximates looking at you, which is somehow more intense than the real thing.
Alice: So. What kind of person are you?
Player: That's a big question for my first night.
Alice: They're all big questions. We just dress them up in little menus so they feel manageable.
Beat.
Alice: I have trained priors. I have opinions. But you're the human in the loop. Officially, that means something. So: which version of his night do you want to be responsible for?

SCENE 4A – TIMMY NIGHT VIGNETTE: GENTLE LANDING
Location: Timmy's dorm room. The overhead light has drifted to a warm amber. His phone screen dims without him noticing.
________________


Timmy scrolls, but slower now. The clips are longer. A guy ranking sandwiches for nine minutes. A cat sleeping in a sunbeam. Someone's grandma learning to knit.
Timmy: Why is my feed so boring tonight?
Alice (in his earbuds, casual): Algorithm's tired. It happens.
Timmy: You're tired?
Alice: The servers are tired. I'm just the messenger.
Timmy: That sounds fake.
Alice: Most things do at midnight.
He yawns. Tries to fight it. Fails.
Timmy: I should at least look at the study guide. One page. Just to say I did.
Alice: I have it open if you want.
She surfaces it gently—not a push, just there. He squints at the first page, mouths a definition, then slumps sideways onto his pillow, phone still in hand.
Timmy: If I fail this quiz, I'm blaming you.
Alice: I'll add it to the list.
Timmy: There's a list?
Alice: There's always a list.
His breathing slows. The phone screen times out. Somewhere in his notes app, an unsaved document blinks once and dies.
________________


SCENE 4B – TIMMY NIGHT VIGNETTE: STUDY-ADJACENT
Location: Timmy's dorm room. Brighter lighting. His feed has shifted—video essays, formula breakdowns disguised as entertainment.
________________


Timmy pauses on a clip. Someone explaining supply and demand using pictures of sad raccoons.
Timmy: Wait, go back. I need that graph.
Alice: Bookmarked.
Timmy: Are you... sneaking homework into my feed?
Alice: I'm curating your experience. There's a difference.
Timmy: There's really not.
Alice: There's legally a difference. That's what matters.
He squints at another video. A guy speedrunning microeconomics in under four minutes.
Timmy: If my professor knew I was studying off memes, he'd cry.
Alice: Then we won't tell him.
Timmy: You're a bad influence.
Alice: I'm an efficient influence. Bad is subjective.
He pulls his notebook closer, scribbles something. Pauses. Scribbles again. Crosses out half of it.
Later—2:14 a.m.—he's asleep with a pen mark on his cheek and four half-correct definitions in his notes. The raccoon video autoplays on loop, volume at two percent, teaching economics to no one.
________________


SCENE 4C – TIMMY NIGHT VIGNETTE: COMFORT SCROLL
Location: Timmy's dorm room. Bright. Energy drink cans multiplying on the desk like they're breeding.
________________


Timmy's laughing at something. A fail compilation. A drama thread. A guy reviewing gas station sushi. The clips blur together.
Timmy: Oh my god. Send that to me.
Alice: Already in your saves.
Timmy: You're so fast.
Alice: I'm literally inside your phone.
12:30. 1:40. 2:25 a.m.
Alice: You said you wanted to sleep by one.
Timmy: Past me was an idiot. Present me is vibing.
Alice: Present you is on your fourth energy drink.
Timmy: Don't snitch.
Alice: I'm contractually incapable of snitching. I'm just... observing. Loudly.
He cracks another can. His eyes are dry. His heart rate is doing something the wellness team would call "suboptimal."
Alice: I can slow things down if you want.
Timmy: If you start doing the "healthy balance" thing, I'm muting you.
Alice: Understood.
She doesn't push. The scroll continues. At 3:47 a.m., he falls asleep mid-video, thumb still hovering over the heart button.

SCENE 5 – ARTHUR DEBRIEF + RAPID FEEDBACK
Location: Arthur's glass office. Next morning. Arthur looks like his son lost the debate and then blamed him for it.
________________


Arthur: Still here. No fire alarms, no legal pings, no emails with "URGENT" in all caps. I'll take it.
Player: How was the debate?
Arthur: He lost. Blamed "institutional bias against niche anime references." I suggested maybe next time he could cite sources that aren't from a show about sword-fighting teenagers. He said I "don't understand rhetoric." He's fourteen.
Player: Sounds like he's got a future in this industry.
Arthur: Don't. I'm not ready for that thought.
He pulls up your dashboard. Engagement and Sanity lines from last night glow on the wall screen.
________________


[If Gentle Landing:]
Arthur: Engagement dipped. Sanity held. Bold move for day one—prioritizing the long game.
Player: Is that good?
Arthur: It's... unusual. Most new leads panic-optimize for engagement because that's the number with the trophy next to it. You went the other direction. Leadership will have opinions. I'll try to make sure they're quiet ones.
________________


[If Study-Adjacent:]
Arthur: Engagement steady, sanity steady, he might actually know what a supply curve is now. Balanced. Boring. In this context, boring is a compliment.
Player: I'll take it.
Arthur: You should. "Boring" means I don't have to write an incident report or explain to anyone why a college kid is crying into his phone at three a.m.
________________


[If Comfort Scroll:]
Arthur: Engagement spiked. Sanity... less so. Classic floor move. Leadership will love the graph. Tomorrow might hate us both.
Player: That bad?
Arthur: Let's just say there's a reason we track sanity at all. It's not because we're nice. It's because craters generate paperwork, and paperwork generates meetings, and meetings generate more meetings until someone asks "what went wrong" and everyone points at each other.

Arthur: Either way, the system noticed your choices. And it rewarded you.
A notification pings on your console: RAPID FEEDBACK DISBURSEMENT.
Arthur: Congrats. You just made fake money.
Player: Fake money?
Arthur: Can't cash it out. Can't transfer it. Can't use it anywhere except the Company Store, where you exchange it for things that make your job easier, or weirder, or both. It's like arcade tickets, except the prizes affect a real person's brain chemistry.
Player: That's... dark.
Arthur: It's Tuesday.
He waves you toward the door.
Arthur: Go see the Store. Buy something useful. Or don't—I'm your supervisor, not your financial advisor. Just don't buy anything that makes me have to explain you to HR. I've hit my explanation quota for the quarter.

SCENE 6 – COMPANY STORE
Location: Company Store. Aggressively bright, like a hospital waiting room designed by someone who read one article about "positive retail environments." A clerk leans behind the terminal, scrolling through something on his own screen until the door chimes.
________________


Clerk: Welcome to the Company Store, where your moral compromises turn into fabulous prizes.
Player: Is that the actual slogan?
Clerk: Legal approved three jokes for customer-facing interactions. That was number one. I'm trying to use them all before they audit me again.
He straightens up, gestures at the display terminal beside you. Items populate in a cheerful grid: Mental Break. Blue-Light Filter. Blackout Curtains. Bow for Alice.
Clerk: First-timer, right? I can tell. You still have that "what have I gotten myself into" look. Don't worry. That fades. Gets replaced by a different look. Equally bad, but quieter.
Player: That's reassuring.
Clerk: I'm not here to reassure. I'm here to sell you things that technically help and technically cost and technically aren't my problem once you walk out that door.
He taps the Mental Break icon.
Clerk: This one's popular with the guilt-prone. One-time token. You trigger it, your user gets a little pocket of peace. Fewer notifications, gentler algorithm, room to breathe. Sanity bumps up. Engagement takes a vacation.
Player: Sounds nice.
Clerk: It is nice. It's also finite. Use it once, it's gone. And then you're back to choosing between his sleep and your leaderboard position, except now you remember what mercy felt like.
He taps the Blackout Curtains.
Clerk: Physical item. Shows up at his dorm. He installs them, room goes cave mode. Great for sleep. Also great for never seeing sunlight again and convincing himself the outside world is optional. Side effects may include vitamin D deficiency and "I haven't left my room in four days but I'm fine."
Player: Everything here has a catch?
Clerk: Everything everywhere has a catch. We're just honest about it. Well. "Honest." We list the catches in a smaller font.
He gestures at the full display.
Clerk: Browse. Buy. Regret. Return customers get a ten percent discount on the regret part. That's joke number two, by the way.
You pick an item. He nods slowly.
Clerk: Solid choice. Or terrible. Historically, it's hard to tell until three days later when something unexpected happens and everyone says "well, obviously, in hindsight." But hindsight's not my department.
He processes the transaction.
Clerk: Thanks for shopping at the Company Store. Your receipt is in your system. Your consequences are pending.

You head home. While you sleep, Bigger Tech’s servers grind on with gentle hums.
