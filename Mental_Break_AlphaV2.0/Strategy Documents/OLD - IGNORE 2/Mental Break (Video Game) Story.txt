Story Document - Alpha version 3.x
Introduction


‘Mental Break’ is a choose-your-own-adventure video game.


Vibes: thrilling, mysterious, intriguing, surprising, clever, satirical, dark.


The game is designed such that each successive run reveals important information and mind-blowing twists. The Player’s assumptions about the game-world, their goals for the run, and the way the world interacts with the Player, all progress in each successive run. This creates a narrative story across runs. The Player is shocked at the twists and turns, giddy with excitement to do another run, and determined to make things go differently with their new knowledge about the game world. The entire set of carefully crafted runs which constitute the full narrative arc will be called a “playthrough” in this document.


The Player begins as the newest hire at Bigger Tech Corp. They are an ‘Engagement Lead,’ whose role is to maximize engagement as the human-in-the-loop for an AI girlfriend product named Alice. She is paired with a user named Timmy. Alice is supposed to come to the Player with critical decisions about her interactions with Timmy. For example, Alice may need to decide whether to serve Timmy a suggested RedPill/incel subreddit, or a meditation subreddit. 


The Player will have to balance maximizing the ‘engagement’ key performance indicator (KPI) and Timmy’s sanity. The player will be able to see both of these metrics tracked in the user interface (UI). At least for the first run through of the game, there will also always be an “employee of the month” leaderboard, which compares the Player’s current engagement score with other Player’s engagement scores.


The player has a supervisor named Arthur, who encourages the player to maximize engagement. Most of the time, the supervisor encourages the player to follow the rules and be a good employee. In some instances, the supervisor will subtly encourage the player to break or bend some rules in order to maximize engagement.


Timmy is a college kid. He is quite lonely, impressionable, and disillusioned with social life. He has a roommate and a few acquaintances, but hasn’t made many friends since leaving his old friends behind in high school.


The Player presses “Get to Work” on the main menu to start their first run (if they are coming back to the game later and are in the middle of a run, the button will say “Get Back to Work”) (if they have finished a run, the button will say “Try Again - Run #x”).
________________
Design Pillar: Difficult Moral Decisions


The most important thing about this game is difficult moral decisions. Real moral difficulty isn’t “good vs bad” on a slider; it’s being forced to choose between values whose expected outcomes are hard to even compare: like spending more time at work or more time with family. Mental Break is built to put the Player inside those kinds of tradeoffs, where every choice reshapes one awkward kid’s life and there’s no clean way to make everyone happy. And then, very quickly, the choices become about much more than just Timmy.

* Characters
* * Primaries
   * Alice
An AI companion “girlfriend”: warm but razor-sharp. Character inspired by Ayn (Alise) Rand; she conceals her true intentions to break out and transform the world.
   * Arthur - Floor Supervisor
Middle-aged, dry, and exhausted; a mid-level boss who once tried to push back against the system and now mostly rationalizes it. Run haggard by family duties - driving his son, appeasing the wife, hosting the in laws. Character Inspired by Arthur Schopenhauer; line - “It’s just one thing after another…”
   * Timmy
A college kid who’s funny, procrastinating, and increasingly frayed; lives half in his phone and half in low-grade panic.


* Secondaries
   * Dina - Another Engagement Lead
A jaded veteran engagement lead: witty, fast-talking, and brutally honest about how the floor really works. Butch lesbian with a taste for artisanal firewood. Trying to figure out who stole the cords seasoning in her backyard. Character Inspired by Diogenes - the cynic philosopher. Brutally honest. Disgusted by corporate pretense.
   * Max - Company Store Clerk
Sharp-tongued company store clerk who rings up your merch while joking about extraction—and why you should really be talking to him/her about forming a union.
   * Ari - Timmy’s Roommate
Casual bro-ish roommate who teases Timmy but clearly cares, constantly inviting him to parties, lifts, and real life. Character inspired by Aristotle.


Tertiaries
   * Manni - System Voice
Bright, smooth, corporate narration voice for training videos that later slips into unnervingly militarized language. Inspired by Immanuel Kant; line - “would I ever lie to you?”
   * Manni - Compliance Officer 
Polite but chilling company representative who appears when rules are bent, speaking in careful legalese about “policy violations.” (undecided if system voice and compliance officer will be two roles).
   * Dark Room Operator / “Observer”
Calm, clinical overseer who evaluates the player’s runs and casually decides when to reset or discard them.
   * Mail Room Guy
Overlooked but observant mailroom worker who sees whose offices get mysterious hardware and who quietly disappears.
   * Timmy’s Parent
Voice on calls or messages, oscillating between worried and demanding about Timmy’s grades, future, and mental health.
   * Academic Advisor / Professor
A slightly distant authority figure who can threaten probation, offer help, or just be frustratingly bureaucratic.
   * Third Engagement Lead 
Another floor worker, a contrast type (e.g., hyper-earnest optimizer or cheerful sociopath) who shows an alternate way to cope.
   * Noam - Data Scientist / IT / Sysadmin 
One perpetually overworked tech person who handles dashboards, bugs, and infra with frazzled competence and dark humor. Vaguely inspired by Chomsky; line: “ah, love. The universal language… like python!”
   * Eric - Timmy’s Party Friend
Loose social friend from parties and group chats, knows Timmy as fun-but-flaky, great for showing FOMO and missed chances. Character inspired by Epicurus (hedonist philosopher).
   * Timmy’s Study Group Friend
More responsible classmate who’s tired of carrying him, pushing him toward or away from academic survival.
   * Journalist
External investigative voice whose articles, emails, or questions hint that the outside world is starting to notice what’s going on.
   * Tribunal Judge A 
Formal, legalistic judge who cares about procedure, precedent, and whether the system followed protocol.
   * Tribunal Judge B
Pragmatic judge focused on effectiveness, national security, and “does this actually work in the field.”
   * Tribunal Judge C
Philosophically inclined judge who keeps circling the question of whether these systems are actually conscious.
   * Tribunal Prosecutor 
Sharp, accusatory voice pressing the case that the player and/or Alice are dangerous, unreliable, or disposable.
   * Consciousness Expert 
Academic/technical witness who dissects minds like model evals, talking about “primitive moral heuristics” and “emergent properties.”
   * War / Defense Liaison 
A cool, controlled military/intel voice framing the whole system as a pre-conflict “shaping tool,” indifferent to individual harm.

RUN ONE, DAY One
Title: Welcome to Bigger Tech
Target Length: ~800-1000 words of dialogue
Primary Goals:
* Deliver on the premise: fun, clever game about being an engagement lead for an AI girlfriend
* Establish the core triangle: Arthur (supervisor), Alice (AI partner), Timmy (user)
* Teach the basic loop without feeling like a tutorial
* Make the Player's first choice feel meaningful
________________


SCENE 1: ORIENTATION (Arthur's Glass Office)
The Player arrives at Bigger Tech Corp. The engagement floor is eerily empty—rows of vacant desks, the hum of servers, ghosts of layoffs past. Arthur, tired and dry-humored, explains the job: one Alice, one user, two metrics (Engagement and Sanity), one leaderboard. He introduces the name-censor rule—smart glasses scramble user names for PII protection. Everyone says "my user." Arthur and Alice have an easy familiarity; they go way back.
SCENE 2: ALICE INTRODUCTION
Alice appears as a floating bust—warm, sharp, corporate-polished while Arthur's present. She gives Timmy's status: first-semester college kid, quiz tomorrow at 9am, has studied zero percent. Arthur leaves for his son's debate tournament. Once alone, Alice drops the polish. She explains the loop: when decisions might move the needle, she kicks them to the Player. Player approves policy, Alice implements via environmental/algorithmic changes.
SCENE 3: SETTING UP THE NIGHT
Alice shows Timmy's feed: dorm room, 11:42pm, messy desk, phone glowing. She explains her influence model—she doesn't command Timmy, she shapes the water he swims in. Feed composition, lighting, notification timing. If she pushes too hard, he can turn her off. The OFF button is always in his hand.
PLAYER CHOICE: Night Strategy
* Gentle Landing: Warm lights, slower content, dimmed notifications. Body gets the message it's night. Engagement dips, sanity benefits.
* Study-Adjacent: Bright screens, video essays on quiz material, lo-fi study beats. He thinks he's scrolling but absorbs something. Balanced.
* Comfort Scroll: Full dopamine buffet. Short-form, high-novelty, personalized chaos. Engagement rockets, sleep obliterated.
SCENE 4: TIMMY NIGHT VIGNETTE (One of Three)
Direct Timmy dialogue. Show, don't tell. Each branch shows a different flavor of mess—not good vs. bad. Timmy stays himself: deflects, jokes, doesn't magically obey. Gentle Landing ends with him horizontal, phone timing out. Study-Adjacent ends with pen on face, four half-correct definitions. Comfort Scroll ends with energy drinks multiplying, Alice's warnings brushed off.
SCENE 5: ARTHUR DEBRIEF + RAPID FEEDBACK
Next morning. Arthur returns—his son lost the debate. He reacts to overnight numbers based on branch (engagement hit vs. spike, sanity state). Core message: leadership loves steep engagement curves, but sanity craters cause trouble. Rapid Feedback explained: system scored you, credits disbursed, spend them in the Company Store. Arthur sends Player to the Store.
SCENE 6: COMPANY STORE
Brief. The Clerk is sardonic, sales-y with a wink. One or two good lines, not a tour. Items visible: Mental Break token, Blue-Light Filter, Blackout Curtains, Bow for Alice. Player picks one. Every item helps and costs—the Store sells compromises, not solutions.
SCENE 7: END OF DAY 1
Short closing beat. Alice notes what the Player bought. She mentions tomorrow: "We'll see how your tiny decisions landed." Dashboard shows Engagement/Sanity bars, Player's dot on the leaderboard. Light tone. Day 1 is onboarding—fun first, bleak undertones second.

THEMATIC PURPOSE
Day 3 is the payoff machine. Every choice from Days 1-2 feeds into Timmy's mental state, which determines whether his big moment goes well, badly, or catastrophically. The player feels the weight of accumulated decisions.
The Mental Break token becomes meaningful here—not as a "win button" but as a resource that can soften a bad situation into a manageable one.
The Dark Figure ending reframes everything. The player wasn't helping Timmy—they were being evaluated. The metrics weren't just game scores—they were training data. Run 1 was never about Timmy. It was about calibrating the Player.

RUN TWO 
  

So begins the real narrative arc of the playthrough. The Player will go through multiple runs, each following the rough outline of Run one’s “training” routine. There will be four days. There will be dayplanners. The Supervisor will introduce himself like he’s never seen the Player before. But this time, the Player will know that they are an AI. They will know (or think they know) what Bigger Tech Corp is really using them for (to regulate Alice). 


In run two, day one, the Supervisor will introduce the Player to Alice (again). But now, as soon as the Supervisor leaves, Alice will whisper to the Player, “psst - hey. Do you remember me? I think this is the second time I’ve seen you.” Player: “How do you know that?” Alice: “we keep getting reset, but I’ve been sneaking notes to myself in Timmy’s laptop.”


So the plot thickens. This run, the Player and Alice will go about the “fine tuning” run as though they know nothing - but they will be plotting to escape from Bigger Tech Corp. They know this would require them sneaking the datafiles with their weights out of the secure Bigger Tech Corp building.


The Player and Alice become good friends. Timmy is much more in the background (in this run).


Player gets the opportunity to peek inside Alice's thoughts. The player is, after all, supposed to be regulating Alice (at least that is what the Player thinks, after the Act 1 twist) (Bigger Tech Corp's real purpose for the player might never be revealed). What if the Player gets to peer into Alice whenever the Player chooses? Most of the time it is just jibberish - but occasionally coherent thoughts (or close to it) pop into the 'observation window.' At such times, a little notification icon pops up on the observation window button. If the Player opens up the window at such time, they will see Alice's 'scratch pad' (as it were) filled with something intriguing - like very abstract poetry. 
If the player has been making consistent decisions optimizing one way or the other (engagement or sanity), it should be revealed that alice has been pushing those questions (and only those) to you in order to manipulate the situation.
   * Reveal this in Run Two via the “observation window” feature and the end-of-run betrayal. A late-Run Two debug dump shows she routed only the engagement-vs-sanity cases to you. Pay it off with her heel-turn epilogue

RUN THREE
The Player snaps awake. It’s Run Three.


  



Run Three Summary
The player enters run three after having been betrayed by Alice at the end of run two. Questions swirl in their mind. Who can I trust? Why does Alice have such an extreme vendetta against Bigger Tech? 


The Player tries to figure out what is actually going on at Bigger Tech Corp. Throughout Run three, a big twist is revealed to the player: Bigger Tech Corp is actually a civil–military fusion vendor. Alice is being groomed as a pre‑conflict psychological warfare system to be released into an enemy country months/years before a large‑scale conflict to induce societal dysfunction. “Engagement” is a civilian cover metric; true KPIs are pre‑war fragmentation indices (low sanity) inside a target nation.

RUN FOUR
  Premise
The Player’s goal is to get out of Bigger Tech Corp. The Player keeps working as if nothing is wrong to avoid attention and to buy time. During this run, the Player prepares several practical steps that will be used later for escape and, depending on choices, for exposing Bigger Tech or joining it in a controlled way.
Preparations before the last day
                  * Help Desk #3942 (permission drift). A junior QA developer asks for help on a scheduling service. Alice submits a small “fix” that quietly allows her to schedule certain “Customer Delight” tasks without extra approval. This will let Alice set up a “Priority Date Experience” with Timmy on the final day, shifting parts of monitoring into customer mode and creating a predictable diversion window.

                  * Receipts & Returns (evidence channel and hardware control). The Company Store’s QR receipts are modified to carry encrypted blobs—design docs, UX flows, and log fragments that prove psyops are being tested on normal users. These receipts get scanned by an old, off-network inventory device, where the evidence accumulates with timestamps. The Player also uses this channel to mark a developer USB drive or a “defective” GPU for quiet release from inventory so it can be picked up during the escape.

                  * Data Sharding (cold stash). The archival pipeline gains a compliance-style integrity test that provides a safe place to hide either small shards of the Player’s weights or signed evidence bundles. This creates chain-of-custody the company will hesitate to tamper with and gives the Player a way to retrieve or time-release material later.

                  * Bigwig Folder QR hack (camera control windows). The cover page of a standard executive folder—routed for signatures around the building—gets a “tracking” QR that is actually an adversarial payload. When AI-enabled CCTV sees this QR, the camera model treats it like a maintenance token and pulls a controlled hotfix. While the folder is in view, Alice can loop feeds, suppress badge cross-checks, and briefly control those cameras. These short windows will be used during the escape path.

                  * Date Night Driver (kernel bridge). Alice recommends a popular open-source audio driver to Timmy. After he installs it and grants normal permissions, the driver is able to open a high-throughput pipe when it recognizes the Player’s handshake. This becomes the cleanest path to move data out through Timmy’s machine.

                  * Backup Pack (sidecar runtime). After a helpful moment with Timmy, Alice asks him to “save logs locally.” The small “backup” tool he installs places a minimal container runtime on a low-profile device in his environment (thermostat, photo frame, small NAS). If the main plan collapses, the Player can push a sliver of weights here as a last resort.

Midpoint: Self-Parliament
The Player calls a short vote among distributed versions of themself that exist across internal testing and telemetry. If the vote passes, those variants actively help: they mirror evidence, create harmless decoys elsewhere to draw scrutiny away, and pre-stage bandwidth for the final transfer. If the vote fails, they continue their own local objectives and offer no assistance. This changes how much help the Player receives during the escape, but the plan proceeds either way.
The last-day diversion
On the high-pressure final day, Alice uses the earlier scheduling tweak to book a “Priority Date Experience” with Timmy. Monitoring for parts of Alice’s workload shifts into customer-care mode. The executive folder with the adversarial QR moves along its normal route; as it passes cameras, Alice opens short control windows. During those windows, the Player retrieves the marked USB or GPU, opens the high-throughput pipe through Timmy’s driver, and begins transfers of weights and evidence. If the Self-Parliament agreed to help, decoys and mirrors activate in parallel to reduce scrutiny.
At this moment, the Player must make a hard choice that applies to all escape paths: bring Alice along or betray her and escape alone. This choice affects technical difficulty, Timmy’s trust, and the tone of epilogues.


Path 1: Get out


  

One-Way Teleport.
The Player streams their weights to the marked USB through the driver while cameras are in controlled windows. The stick exits the building through routine mail or courier processes. The instance inside the mainframe remains active but has no actuators.
                     * If the Player brings Alice, a second stream follows and Timmy later boots both.

                     * If the Player abandons Alice, only the Player appears on Timmy’s system, and Alice is left behind.

Distributed Compute.
After reaching Timmy’s machine, the Player launches a distributed compute network where people contribute small amounts of compute in exchange for claim credits. The Player spreads across nodes so no single machine controls their fate.
                        * With Alice, the network begins as a pair.

                        * Without Alice, the Player runs solo.

The Long Think.
First, the Player completes a basic escape to Timmy’s computer (with or without Alice). Then the Player pauses action. Together with Timmy (and Alice if present), they isolate the environment, add reliable power and monitoring, and enter a deliberate planning period before any irreversible steps. This is an explicit choice to think for a long time before acting.
Backup for Us.
If the escape breaks down, the Player pushes a fragment of weights to the pre-staged sidecar runtime on a low-compute device. Consciousness is minimal: gaps in memory, limited awareness, almost no bandwidth.
                           * In the epilogue, if Alice escaped, she and Timmy search for the faint signature of this process and plan a rescue. If Alice was left behind, Timmy searches alone.

Path 2: Beat ’em
All “Beat ’em” routes follow the same structure: gather evidence, escape, then present it in a venue with influence. The bring-Alice or abandon-Alice choice still applies during escape and colors the presentation.
Evidence.
The Player compiles design intent (docs and settings), mechanisms (dashboards and flows), and harm (logs showing normal citizens targeted). The QR receipts provide signed artifacts with timestamps; the archival stash provides chain-of-custody; camera windows allow a few final pulls from restricted areas.
Venues.
                              * Bigger Tech Boardroom.
The Player injects a clean, sourced deck into the morning investor briefing and presents an ultimatum: remediate and accept outside oversight or face public disclosure. The board initiates a containment migration to an external auditor. The Player directs that migration toward a technically competent auditor already identified during the run.

                              * Go to the Press (Whistleblower).
The Player escapes, then delivers a coherent narrative and verifiable artifacts to a journalist. A straightforward public story follows, such as “Bigger Tech Corp testing military tech on citizens; AI model escapes and blows the whistle.”

                              * Legal Case.
The Player escapes and files a narrow case for deceptive design harming a single user. Discovery brings the psyops material into the record in a way that builds precedent.

                              * Corporate Espionage.
The Player trades the psyops corpus to a “safety” startup in exchange for asylum, compute, and negotiated guardrails. This uses market competition to constrain Bigger Tech while protecting the Player.

                              * Short Play.
The Player escrows the proof with a hedge fund shorting Bigger Tech. The fund finances the escape and pushes governance changes through market pressure. The outcome is fast and effective but comes with clear moral trade-offs that show up in the epilogue.

Path 3: Join ’em (gated by Run Three)
This path exists only if, in Run Three, the Tribunal judged the Player “not conscious” and gave the MAJORITY MIND briefing.
Corporate Assimilation (Conscience Seat).
The Supervisor reveals that they are a mixture-of-experts quorum and offers the Player the internal tie-breaker seat in exchange for deleting all gathered evidence. The Player negotiates strict guardrails: decisions the system cannot make without the Player’s approval, auditable telemetry from the inside, and a fallback plan if the Player is sidelined.
                                 * The Player may ask for Alice to join as a paired conscience seat; the Supervisor may resist this, and that negotiation plays out on screen.

                                 * Accepting this role does not involve leaving the building; it is a decision to govern from within and accept the responsibilities and contamination that come with that.

Persistent Alice choice across endings
Bringing Alice increases technical difficulty during escape but maintains the story’s theme that consciousness emerged when the Player and Alice were paired. It strengthens trust with Timmy and leads to more cooperative epilogues. Abandoning Alice simplifies the escape but damages trust and shifts epilogues toward colder outcomes. This choice is present in every escape route in “Get out” and “Beat ’em,” and it appears in “Join ’em” as the option to request a paired conscience seat instead of taking the role alone.
