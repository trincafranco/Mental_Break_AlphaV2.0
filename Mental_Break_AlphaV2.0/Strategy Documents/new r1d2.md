### **\[If MISSED – sleep\_in chosen, especially with comfort\_scroll\]:**

**Timmy:** No. No no no—

**Alice (to you):** Heart rate just doubled. He's in full panic mode.

Timmy stops. Stares at her through the glass. His hand reaches for the door handle, then freezes.

**Alice (to you):** He's been standing there for fifteen seconds. Cortisol is spiking. He's not going in.

Inside, the professor glances toward the window—that brief, disappointed teacher look that says "I noticed you, and I'm choosing not to make it a thing."

Timmy's hand drops. He takes a step back. Then another. Then he's retreating down the hall, finding a bench in a quiet corner.

He pulls out his phone. Opens his email. Types: "Professor, I'm so sorry, I woke up with food poisoning and—"

**Alice (to you):** He's been editing that email for four minutes. He hasn't sent anything.

## **SCENE 3 – ARTHUR MEETING**

**Location:** Arthur's glass office. He's on his phone, looking like a man who has been on his phone for most of his adult life.

---

**Arthur (into phone):** No, the one under the sink. The leaky one. Yes, that one. The one that's been leaking since August.

He waves you in without looking, pointing at the chair across from his desk.

**Arthur (into phone):** Because it's December now and the in-laws are coming and I'd prefer my kitchen not smell like mildew when my mother-in-law arrives.

He pinches the bridge of his nose.

**Arthur (into phone):** The plumber says it's the gasket. Then he says it's the pipe. Then he says it's "the whole situation." That's a direct quote. "The whole situation." Three hundred dollars to diagnose "the whole situation."

A pause. He listens.

**Arthur (into phone):** Thursday. Before Thursday. Ideally today but I've learned not to ask for miracles from anyone who charges by the hour.

He hangs up. Stares at the phone for a moment.

**Arthur:** Plumber.

**Player:** Sounds complicated.

**Arthur:** Everything is complicated when you own a house built in 1987\. My wife's been sending me articles about DIY plumbing. I think it's her way of saying something, but I'm too tired to figure out what.

He pulls up your dashboard. Engagement and Sanity lines from Day 1 glow on the wall screen.

**Arthur:** So. Corporate sent me the overnight digest. They do this thing now where they write personality profiles based on one data point. It's like horoscopes for middle management.

**Player:** What's my horoscope?

**Arthur:** Depends on the day.

---

### **\[PATH A – If Player leaned engagement (comfort\_scroll, or study\_adjacent with engagement-favoring morning)\]:**

**Arthur:** Here's what they see: engagement trending up, sanity... creative. That's the kind of curve that makes a director pause their mid-morning scrolling and think, "This person has potential."

**Player:** Potential for what?

**Arthur:** Unclear. "Potential" is one of those words we use when we're not sure if something is good or bad but we want it to sound optimistic.

He taps to another panel. His eyes flick to the door—just for a moment—then back to you.

**Arthur:** Here's the thing about this place. The system rewards certain behaviors. You can work with that, or you can... not.

He pauses, like he's deciding how much to say.

**Arthur:** People who work with it tend to stick around. People who don't...

He trails off. Picks up his coffee. Changes the subject.

**Arthur:** There's a pilot program. This one makes your metrics more sensitive. Your choices land harder. Feedback comes faster. It's like upgrading from a steering wheel to a joystick.

**Player:** That sounds dangerous.

**Arthur:** Everything's dangerous. This is just a different kind of dangerous with better PowerPoint presentations.

He leans back.

**Arthur:** You opt in, you become the test case. Leadership pays attention. You decline, you stay in the normal queue with everyone else who decided caution was underrated.

**\[CHOICE: Opt in / Decline\]**

**\[If opt in\]:**

**Arthur:** Ambitious. I respect that.

He makes a note.

**Arthur:** You're now a different color on three dashboards. Try not to make me explain you to Compliance. I've exceeded my explanation quota for the quarter.

**\[If decline\]:**

**Arthur:** Cautious. We don't get many of those anymore. Everyone wants to be the meteor. Nobody wants to be the atmosphere that slows the meteor down.

He makes a different note.

**Arthur:** You stay in the normal queue. Steady. Predictable. The kind of employee who doesn't give me paperwork.

---

### **\[PATH B – If Player leaned sanity (gentle\_landing, or study\_adjacent with sanity-favoring morning)\]:**

**Arthur:** Sanity looks neat. Steady line, mild upward trend, nothing that requires emergency meetings. Engagement, though...

He tilts his hand back and forth.

**Arthur:** Leadership likes steep curves. They have a poster somewhere with a hockey stick graph on it. Your line looks more like a... horizontal suggestion.

**Player:** Is that a problem?

**Arthur:** Depends who you ask. Ask me, it's fine. Ask the people who decide whether we have jobs next quarter, it's "concerning."

He leans forward. His eyes flick to the door—just for a moment—then back to you.

**Arthur:** Here's the thing. You can't keep a conscience and this job forever. Not because the job is evil—it's just... the job. The system rewards certain behaviors. You either learn to love those behaviors, learn to fake loving those behaviors, or you burn out.

**Player:** Those are my options?

**Arthur:** You can walk the tightrope. Keep both bars in acceptable ranges if you're clever. It's exhausting and eventually something gives, but some people do it. Or you accept that the playbook exists for a reason and stop fighting the current.

**\[CHOICE: Tightrope / Playbook\]**

**\[If tightrope\]:**

**Arthur:** You're going to give me gray hairs.

**Player:** You already have gray hairs.

**Arthur:** I have gray hairs because of people like you. The tightrope walkers. You know what happens to tightrope walkers?

**Player:** They get across?

**Arthur:** Sometimes. Sometimes they fall and make a mess that the rest of us have to clean up. But sure. They get across.

He sighs.

**Arthur:** Good luck. Genuinely.

**\[If playbook\]:**

**Arthur:** Honest answer. I appreciate that.

He nods slowly.

**Arthur:** The playbook isn't perfect. It's not even good. But it's consistent. You follow it, you know what to expect. Nobody's surprised. Nobody's writing memos.

**Player:** That doesn't sound inspiring.

**Arthur:** Inspiration is expensive. Consistency is affordable.

## **SCENE 5B – OFFICE HOURS**

**Location:** Your console. Timmy's in his room, lying on his bed, staring at a grade notification on his phone like it's a ransom note.

---

**Alice:** Professor posted office hours. Timmy saw the email, said "lol rip me," and has been dissociating ever since.

**Player:** Did he at least read the whole email?

**Alice:** He read the subject line. Everything after that was handled by his fight-or-flight response, which chose "freeze."

On the feed, Timmy scrolls past the notification. It bobs up again. He scrolls past it again.

**Alice:** He knows office hours exist. He knows his grade is not what he wanted. He is connecting these concepts like someone trying to assemble IKEA furniture without the instructions.

**Player:** Will he go?

**Alice:** Not without a push. I can handle the logistics—draft an email, grab a time slot, send him walking directions. Or I can mention it once and watch him choose avoidance like it's an Olympic sport.

**\[CHOICE: Push hard / Stay hands-off\]**

**\[If push hard\]:**

**Alice:** Strong-arming it is.

Cut to: Timmy walking into the professor's office. His body language radiates discomfort—shoulders hunched, backpack clutched like a shield.

The professor doesn't look up from his laptop.

**Professor:** Name?

**Timmy:** Uh. Tim. Timothy. Kowalski.

**Professor:** Kowalski. Quiz scores.

He types something. The keyboard sounds like judgment.

**Professor:** You're behind.

**Timmy:** I know. I'm trying to—

**Professor:** Have a seat.

Timmy sits on the edge of the chair, knees together, like he's waiting in the principal's office.

The professor finally looks up. Peers over his glasses. The look isn't mean—it's worse. It's the look of someone who has had this conversation four hundred times.

**Professor:** What happened?

**Timmy:** I don't... I studied. Kind of. I thought I knew it.

**Professor:** But you didn't.

**Timmy:** No. I guess not.

Silence. The professor waits. Timmy shifts in his seat.

**Timmy:** Is there, like... is there anything I can do? To bring my grade up?

**Professor:** There's the next quiz. There's the midterm. There's the final. Those are the things you can do.

**Timmy:** Right. Okay.

**Professor:** Office hours are Tuesdays and Thursdays. Bring specific questions. Not "I don't understand." Specific.

**Timmy:** Okay.

**Professor:** Is there anything else?

There's nothing else. There was never anything else. Timmy was hoping for something—guidance, maybe, or acknowledgment that he's a person and not just a data point—but the professor is already looking at his laptop again.

**Timmy:** No. Thanks.

He leaves. The door clicks shut behind him like a period at the end of a sentence.

Cut back to: Your console.

**Alice:** He hated every second. But he went.

**Player:** Is that good?

**Alice:** It's forward motion. Forward motion beats spiraling in place.

**\[If stay hands-off\]:**

**Alice:** Hands-off it is.

**Alice:** And there it goes. Buried under seventeen other apps and the general noise of being alive.

**Player:** Will he regret that?

**Alice:** Probably. But not today. Today he's choosing the version of himself that doesn't have to deal with it.

She pauses.

**Alice:** It's a very human choice. I'm not sure I'd do it differently.
